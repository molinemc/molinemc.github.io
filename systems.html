SYNTHETIC-COHORT SITE EVALUATION

MEAS Sole architect & engineer 2026

Spins up varied personas that act as potential customers browsing a website. Each persona has distinct demographics, goals, budget, expertise, and patience, and browses a website the way a human does; based on this context, it makes its own decisions to purchase, contact, continue, or abandon. This reveals how and why a website might fail customers without paying for a human focus group. For example, a retiree who just wants a phone number and a procurement officer looking for a spec sheet would move on for different reasons; the comprehensive report provides the trajectory of each, showing what they were looking for and how quickly they found it or stopped looking. Reports also describe evidence boundaries: widgets the cohort could not operate, broken links encountered, and pages that were never found. These findings help understand limits of evaluation, but also where usage patterns might not match expectations. Language models reason about pages and choose each persona's actions; the trajectories and aggregate results are computed from those recorded actions, not written by a model, so they can't be hallucinated. Rather than scraping web pages, this tool renders content using Chromium, so no new code is required for different sites.

Cost. A run has to come in under five dollars, which rules out having a model reason over every page of a large site and forces the agent to spend its budget where a real visitor would. Cost, run time and persona behavior bounded against real sites: run across roughly fifty sites in repeated batches to test generality. The batch below is one representative sample.
Findings and sample reports

What the batch showed

Three results were about the sites rather than the tool. dev.to produced no immediate signups, though two of three personas said they would come back later — a genuine result, not a scoring bug. Client A's single non-conversion traced to real product fit, price and dietary restrictions, not to a site or tool failure. And two Client B personas flagged real policy terms, final-sale and frequent stock-outs, as concerns for future purchases.

The rest were about the tool's own limits, which it reported rather than papering over. On Ghost, Vercel and Client C it flagged specific elements as outside what it could operate — a dead search box, a free-text AI widget, a store locator and an off-site spec sheet — instead of skipping them silently. Chewy and NPR it assessed as prohibitively defended before any persona ran, at no cost. On Letterboxd a CAPTCHA stopped the one attempt, and the run was flagged for human review rather than guessed at.

Two reports from that batch, unedited. Worth looking at: the per-persona question table, which separates a question the site answered from one it answered satisfactorily, and the list of page elements no visitor discovered.

TAD

EXPLGOALINDIVLEARN Architect & PI 2023–2025

Trustworthy Algorithmic Delegate

Makes medical triage decisions that match a specific clinician's judgment rather than a population average. Performs human-like reasoning to evaluate symptoms and possible causes, compare to similar cases, consider prognostic information, and judge priorities. I built this system as a principal investigator on DARPA's In the Moment program, acting as lead developer and directing a team of ten spread across four institutions. 

Medical triage is particularly difficult for AI because experts disagree on the right judgements and there is often no correct answer to match against. TAD learned offline to understand the characteristics that real human doctors use to make decisions, so that it could quickly make hard decisions online, just as a real doctor must. 

Two REST/JSON interfaces connected TAD to externally developed systems: the first interface was to the evaluation harness, so that TAD could be evaluated systematically and blindly against questions the developers had never seen. The second interface connected TAD to human-characterization systems built by BBN and SoarTech, for training on human data. I helped define both specifications without owning them.

TAD interoperates with distinguishing characteristics defined by other teams, using their choice of features rather than ours; runs on local machinery; and answers queries in under a second. Delivered on schedule and won every round: targets met or exceeded in all four independent evaluations, run by CACI, ahead of the competing generative-AI team each time, with a system that does not generate.

The evaluation team reported “impressive progress” from two alignable decision-makers, one of them TAD.Jennifer McVay (CACI) and Ewart de Visser, AAAI Fall Symposium 2025

MILITARY CAMPAIGN PLANNER

GOALREPAIR Component architect 2022–2024

DARPA SCEPTER program

A goal-based planner built for a program whose point was speed: machine-generated strategies produced fast enough to outpace human planners. The distinguishing requirement was scale — it had to plan over large numbers of units, where hierarchical decomposition earns its keep and flat search does not. It ran as a component of a larger simulation system built by others, connecting over an MQTT interface, so the two could be developed and deployed independently.

At the leaves the planner had to issue direction in an idiosyncratic narrative-oriented language to guide subagents. Results could not be accurately anticipated before execution, and needed approximation. Won and delivered inside a $4.4M program: I co-wrote the winning proposal and led this component's design, working with two Parallax scientists and three from SIFT.
Parallax awarded DARPA's SCEPTER program contract, May 2023 CUI; description limited to what is public

PRIDE

EXPLINDIV Architect 2019–2023

Prediction and Recognition of Intent, Decision and Emotion

A decision aid for weighing international consequences: it represents what another party wants, what they will and will not do, and what they think of everyone else at the table. Emotions are modeled rather than abstracted away, on the argument that an action giving offense is a fact about the world a planner has to account for. I won this DARPA program for Knexus before leaving for Parallax, then subcontracted back to lead its design; most of it was implemented at Knexus. Architect, never PI. Exercised against two historical cases: a Syria war-game analysis and the Cuban Missile Crisis. Repeated Monte Carlo runs tested generalization of the knowledge representation to related cases.

Ran on a standard laptop: theory-of-mind reasoning with higher-order probabilistic and cultural modeling, inside a laptop's computational budget, with the architecture redesigned in phase two around the people using it.
Knexus and Parallax advance DARPA's PRIDE project, Aug 2023 export-controlled; description limited to what is public

GATE

EXPLGOALREPAIR Architect & engineer 2018–2019

Goal-based team crisis intervention

Watches a human team pursuing a shared goal, recognizes crises, and proactively recommends a responsive, small change to the plan. Critical to this is maintaining structure: when one teammate's part of a distributed shared plan changes, other teammates may fail due to dependencies. GATE reassigns lower-level goals in such a way that the overall dependency structure is preserved, without communication. GATE is built on ARTUE, with state estimation, crisis recognition and intervention generation as three separable subproblems.

Builds onARTUE, SHOP2-PDDL+

Interventions are generated by a new planner, Goal Assignment Structure Refinement (GASR). I built GASR by adapting the action implementation of SHOP2‑PDDL+ to a goal decomposition algorithm. By delegating lower-level planning to subteams with separate resources, GASR greatly speeds planning and replanning. Follow-on: I rebuilt GASR from scratch in Rust in 2026; no new results published.

It had to be usable by soldiers in the field without pulling attention off the task they were already doing, and reading a full plan would cost attention nobody had to spare. Teams responded faster and got more done: significantly quicker to both complications (p<.01) and significantly more effective. GASR held search effort roughly flat where full hierarchical planning for the combined team peaked near 900 nodes — it plans less because it delegates more.
Goal-based Team Crisis Intervention, Advances in Cognitive Systems 9, 2020

CARPE / AMORE

CONTREPAIR Architect & engineer 2013–2017

Cognitive Architecture for RePlanning and Execution

Builds onSHOP2-PDDL+, in its first version

Plans and schedules naval mine countermeasures operations, adapting a long-horizon plan as conditions, assets and objectives shift. Rebuilt three years later as AMORE, a second version under a second contract, whose construction and evaluation I managed across a team of four.

Dozens of units over operations lasting weeks, with each class of unit carrying its own downtime and transit requirements — so the schedule had to stay feasible against heterogeneous constraints.  Existing planners would create schedules from scratch, and humans wouldn't use the new, radically different plans. CARPE and AMORE were designed for "minimal disruption", meaning that they would deliberately find a plan as similar as possible to the original plan that accounted for new shifts.  Its scheduling algorithm, CLOSR, repaired plans with minimal disruption and still met the mission standard across a series of randomly generated operations. The approach outlasted the contract that produced it, and outlasted me: the Navy funded a second generation rather than a follow-on study, and the work carried on after I left in 2018.
Minimally Disruptive Schedule Repair for MCM Missions, Proc. SPIE 9454, 2015PDF

ASM

EXPL Architect & engineer 2014–2016

Autonomous Squad Member

An agent that accompanies a team on a mission, works out what the team is doing when goals change, and keeps up by planning for a matching goal. Five components in one decision cycle: it parses what teammates say out loud, explains what must have happened to make that utterance true, recognizes the plan the team has adopted, selects a goal that fits, and plans for it. Builds on DiscoverHistory, SHOP2-PDDL+, and ARTUE. Integrates with components created by teammates for natural language interpretation and plan recognition. Runs against a Havok 3D simulation.

Builds onDiscoverHistory, ARTUE, SHOP2-PDDL+

ASM does not communicate directly with human teammates, who might be operating quietly in hostile territory. Everything it knows comes from watching, and listening to brief speech acts between human team members. Demonstrated in multiple reconnaissance scenarios. Example: team is interrupted by a sniper, abandons the mission goal for cover and counter-fire, clears the threat and resumes. Agent tracks, recognizes intent of squad members, and cooperates toward shared goals without communicating. 
Goal Reasoning for an Autonomous Squad Member, ACS Goal Reasoning Workshop 2015

ADRUS

REPAIR Architect & engineer 2013–2015

Anomaly Detection and Recovery for Unmanned Systems

Builds onSHOP2-PDDL+, in its first version

Detects when something has gone wrong on an unmanned vehicle mid-mission and replans to recover, so a person supervises rather than drives. The problem it was built for is labor, not autonomy for its own sake: unmanned systems of the period ran teleoperated, several operators to a vehicle, which caps how many you can field at all. Moving routine anomaly handling onto the vehicle inverts that ratio.

Funded past the prototype: recovery from unexpected events demonstrated in simulated logistics and resupply missions, then carried through to a Phase II development contract.
ONR/OSD SBIR Phase II, contract N00014-14-C-0279 — no public report; SBIR data rights

Foundations

5 systems

These foundations are general solutions for a category of problems. Requirements come from the space of covered domains rather than from one. Domain generality is the binding constraint: no knowledge of a particular world may be written in program code, only in logic-based models the system reads. The model language has to absorb variability nobody has specified yet, and methods must absorb the flexibility of that language. All of the following tools are interoperable with a single language, an extension of PDDL+. The applications above draw on these: ARTUE in GATE and ASM, DiscoverHistory in ASM, and SHOP2‑PDDL+ in four of them.

DISCOVERHISTORY

EXPL Architect & engineer 2010–2017

Reasons backward from partial observations to what must have happened, over mixed discrete and continuous data. Instead of detecting only that a plan failed, the agent can say which unobserved event explains it. DHAgent puts this to work: belief management sits between monitoring and planning, so the agent plans against what it has inferred rather than only what it has seen.

The same answers at a twentieth of the cost: as accurate as a state-of-the-art deductive method while cutting execution time 95%. An agent using it achieved 20–63% more of its goals than one that does not explain.
DiscoverHistory: Understanding the Past in Planning and Execution, AAMAS 2012PDF Source code (GitLab)

EML

EXPLLEARN Architect & engineer 2011–2017

Explanation-Based Model Learner

Turns explanations of what must have happened into corrections of the agent's own model of its environment. An agent that meets a dynamic nobody told it about stops being repeatedly surprised by it and starts predicting it.

Works withDiscoverHistory

An agent stops being surprised by the same thing twice: within five learning trials it reached 90% of the achievable improvement, learning from a handful of surprises rather than a large dataset.
Understanding What May Have Happened in Dynamic, Partially Observable Environments, George Mason University, 2017

ARTUE

EXPLGOALLEARN Architect & engineer 2009–2013

Autonomous Response to Unexpected Events

A domain-independent agent that decides which goal to pursue when something surprising happens. It explains the discrepancy first, then forms a new goal out of the explanation, rather than replanning toward a goal that may no longer make sense. In one scenario it infers an approaching storm from a single lightning strike, works out that the ship it was told to deliver is now in danger, and adopts sheltering — a goal nobody gave it. Its planner is SHOP2‑PDDL+. A SOAP agent interface allows remote use: observations in, actions out, so the agent could sit somewhere other than the environment it was acting in.

Builds onSHOP2-PDDL+

Every component earns its place, measurably: an ablation across three scenarios, 25 runs each, took scores from 0.33–0.35 planning once, through 0.40–0.48 with replanning and 0.58–0.74 with explanation, to 0.73–0.98 for the whole agent. Every step significant.

ARTUE's main cost is in hand-authored knowledge: a described Navy strategy domain required 115 predicate forms, 148 methods, 9 principles and 28 goal-generation rules. Two successors, built by others under my supervision, went after exactly that. T-ARTUE learns goal selection knowledge by querying a human expert instead of being handed it. M-ARTUE replaces domain-specific goal formulation with two domain-independent motivators that score a goal by its projected consequences, balancing the agent's internal needs against goals introduced from outside. In tests on simulated planetary rovers, M-ARTUE reached its goals as reliably as ARTUE did with hand-written rules.

SHOP2-PDDL+

CONT Architect & engineer 2010

HTN planner with nonlinear continuous effects

The first hierarchical task network planner able to reason about nonlinear continuous effects. A wait action and a state-projection algorithm let it ask what the world will look like at an arbitrary future moment, with fuel draining, vessels moving, and events firing the instant a threshold is crossed. ARTUE plans with it.

Fast enough to plan during execution: fifty-action plans in about a second with a dozen moving vessels. General: all twenty rover benchmark problems solved where the competing planner failed four.
Planning in Dynamic Environments: Extending HTNs with Nonlinear Continuous Effects, AAAI 2010 PDF Source code (GitLab)

ENVIRONMENT TRANSFORMATION FRAMEWORK

MEAS Architect 2019–2023

Deliberately transforms an environment to measure how far an agent degrades, and characterizes the classes of novelty an agent actually meets once deployed. The transformations are defined from the environment's side rather than the agent's, which is what makes the measurement comparable: most definitions call a thing novel when it differs from what some particular agent has seen, so two agents can never face the same novelty unless they have lived the same life. Eight dimensions describe how changes differ — whether the agent steers itself into the change or is dragged in, whether it is noticed before or after it starts to hurt, whether noticing takes deliberate effort.

Robustness claims become testable: the framework fixes the minimum conditions under which a change is worth testing at all — it has to affect performance, be possible to notice, and leave the agent able to respond.
An Environment Transformation-based Framework for Comparison of Open-World Learning Agents, AAAI Spring Symposium 2022

TIELT / LIET

MEAS Architect & engineer 2003–2009

Testbed for Integrating and Evaluating Learning Techniques

The integration layer for a DARPA program, built to turn an M×N interface problem into an M+N one: one interface per decision system (i.e., agent) and one per environment, instead of one for every pairing. It let learning agents written by five to ten independent teams run against simulated environments none of them had built, including a commercial game engine from the studio that became Rockstar New England. I owned the abstraction and the documentation those teams built against. Seven environments sat behind that one abstraction: MadRTS, Unreal Tournament, Stratagus, Chess, Zork, the Rush 2008 football simulator, and Gazebo/Stage. Diverse simple interface types were supported by games of that era, including pipes, formatted messages sent on a TCP stream, Java library APIs, and DLL binding; TIELT has customizable interfaces that read messages from each, and TIELT was configured to support new ones using regular expressions rather than coding. Decision systems mostly wrote TIELT-specific interfaces using one of these interface types; Soar was an exception, where TIELT accommodated an existing Java API.

Experiments had to run without leaking hidden state to the decision systems, or the evaluation would measure nothing. And it had to run locally on every performer's machine — Mac, Linux and Windows — rather than on infrastructure we controlled. Working as intended: a later CaT experiment was set up by adapting the earlier one inside TIELT rather than rebuilding the integration. LIET was a smaller refactor, built with an early standard agent interface. Other groups built on it without me. A Georgia Tech team used TIELT as the interface between their transfer-learning agent and MadRTS, a strategy game built for DARPA by Mad Doc Software, a commercial game designer. Lehigh's InSyTe group published TIELT game model and simulator interface files for Call to Power 2, plus a LIET agent, downloadable from the Lehigh group's site. In 2010 a group at Complutense Madrid, CSIC and Georgia Tech built MMPM as an explicit alternative, comparing to TIELT's action language in particular.
Transfer Learning in Real-Time Strategy Games Using Hybrid CBR/RL, IJCAI 2007 Lehigh InSyTe: CTP2 integration with TIELT and LIET MMPM: a Generic Platform for Case-Based Planning Research, ICCBR 2010 workshop

CAT

LEARN Architect & engineer 2005

Case-based Tactician

Learns to beat an opponent it has never seen, in the real-time strategy game Stratagus, by retrieving remembered cases from past similar positions and adapting the tactic that performed best from those cases. Each remembered case describes the buildings available, units, a tactic, and tactic performance; roughly eight tactic decisions decide a five-minute game.

Stratagus exposed only a high-level interface for expressing preferences over unit behavior, not direct control of individual units. That is why a case selects a tactic rather than a sequence of moves: the grain of the decision was set by what the engine would let an outside system say. Beat opponents it had never seen: the first case-based system to learn to defeat an unknown real-time strategy opponent, matching or beating the best hand-evolved counter-strategy on both victory and score — which no single static strategy managed — though not every counter-strategy individually.
Learning to Win: Case-Based Plan Selection in a Real-Time Strategy Game, IJCAI 2005 workshop ICCBR 2005 version

CASEY / CBQL-PR

LEARN Architect & engineer 2009

Case-Based Q-Lambda with Plan Recognition

A transfer learning system for adversarial environments: it learns to play quarterback against a defense that secretly picks one of five plays each down, transferring what it recognizes about the defense into control of its own side. Rather than learn over the raw movements of eight defenders, it rapidly recognizes the defense's plan by assigning it to an acquired cluster and learns over that instead, collapsing a state space of size 4.3 billion into two features during the learning process.

Learned ten times faster than competing learners without plan recognition: reached its best competitor's peak in a tenth of the time, after recognizing the defense's real play more than 99% of the time within a thousand examples. It beat both baselines in every comparison (p<.0001).
Beating the Defense: Using Plan Recognition to Inform Learning Agents, FLAIRS 2009 Casey’s Quest, IJCAI 2009 Best Overall Video

CASSL

CONTLEARN Architect & engineer 2008

Continuous Action and State Space Learner

Learns what an action will do when both the action and the state are continuous, rather than chopping them into buckets first. Most reinforcement learners at the time discretized the action space to make it tractable; but in true continuous domains, that may miss the best action. CASSL keeps the models continuous and regresses over them when it applies case knowledge.

MadRTS gave orders over continuous parameters — heading, group size and the rest — so a discretized model could not express the order the situation actually called for. Did the job with a tenth of the effort: under ten orders per episode within about 100 training episodes, where a random agent plateaued near 80 and a CMAC controller converged more slowly.
Learning Continuous Action Models in a Real-Time Strategy Environment, FLAIRS 2008
